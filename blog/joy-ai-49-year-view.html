Joy, AI, and the 49-Year View

On making games, the death of skill, fear as a business model, and what joy actually is — written from a studio that still has to ship software on Tuesday.

Shane Foster · Founder, Haven Command LLC

I turned forty-nine this year. There is something that happens when you have watched the world long enough - you stop being surprised. Not because you go numb, I think, but because you have seen enough cycles to start recognizing the shape of things. Once you can see the shape, the surprise goes out of it.

I want to tell you what that shape looks like. Because I think it matters. And because the shape of the past, if you are willing to look at it honestly, tells you a great deal about where you are standing right now.

I have watched personal computing go from a hobbyist curiosity to something three billion people carry in their hands every waking hour. I watched the internet get invented, celebrated, loved, and blamed. The same arc with social media. The same with smartphones. And now, here we are, with artificial intelligence.

Now, here is the interesting thing about that pattern. It never changes. The form changes. The fear does not.

What do forty-nine years of technology actually look like?

Let me tell you what I have actually watched. Not what I was told to believe. What I lived through, in my own body, in real time.

I grew up in the shadow of the Cold War. If you are young enough that this is history to you, sit with it for a second. It was not history to us. I grew up doing duck-and-cover drills at school, and there were adults who went very quiet when certain news came on the radio. The threat of nuclear annihilation was not an abstraction - it was a low hum that went with you everywhere, even on good days. And then - November 9, 1989 - a wall came down. I remember where I was. The fear that had organized everything I had ever known just dissolved, nearly overnight. I still find that extraordinary. I think I always will.

Then, within a decade, the internet arrived. And I believed in it completely - I am not embarrassed to say that. It felt like the most hopeful thing that had ever happened to human beings. All of us, connected, sharing what we knew. The entire accumulated discovery of human civilization available to anyone patient enough to wait for the dial-up to finish. Part of me still believes in that version of what it was supposed to be.

Then came September 11th. I will not try to describe that day - you know it, or you have heard enough. What I want to point at is what happened in the years after. Something broke in the culture that has not healed yet. We decided - not in a meeting, not with a vote, just quietly, collectively - that the world was dangerous. That strangers were threats. That fear was the responsible position. We built that decision into policy, into architecture, into the way we raised the next generation. It did not stay contained. It spread.

The Atari 2600 launched the same year I was born - 1977. Pong had already been running in arcades for five years. I grew up alongside this medium. I watched it go from a curiosity, to a toy, to an art form, to one of the largest entertainment industries on the planet. Games outlasted the Cold War. They survived the dot-com crash, September 11th, the Great Recession, a global pandemic. They are still here. They keep growing.

I tell you this because the world is consistently painted as a landscape of death and misery and constant threat. And yet the things that give people genuine joy - connection, play, discovery, story - are more accessible now than at any point in history. Both of those things are true at the same time. But only one of them gets the airtime. Does that make sense?

"Every generation inherits the fear of the one before it. The question is not whether you received it. The question is whether you decide to keep it."

Why are people really against AI?

Here is what I notice about the people who are loudest against artificial intelligence. Most of them are not worried about ethics. Not really, not at the core. They are worried their skill has become irrelevant. And I say this with full compassion, because I have felt that same vertigo myself. You spend years - real years, real effort, mornings you gave up, things you missed - becoming good at something genuinely difficult. Drawing. Writing. Composing. Coding. And then something arrives - ChatGPT, Midjourney, Copilot - that produces a credible version of that thing in thirty seconds.

That is not just a professional problem. It cuts at something much deeper - at whether the thing you built your life around still means something. When Hollywood writers and actors walked out in 2023, people called it a contract dispute. It was not. It was an identity crisis wearing the costume of a labor negotiation. I understand that. I have been there, in my own quieter way.

And the fear is not paranoia. The credible version is often good enough. Not for every purpose - but for enough purposes that the economics have already shifted. The client who cannot distinguish between a carefully composed illustration and a generated one will choose the generated one, because it is cheaper and faster. The company that needs five hundred marketing emails does not need to hire a writer for them. The studio that once needed twelve months and twenty people can ship something playable in three months with four. These are not hypotheticals. They are already the world.

So when someone tells you the fear is overblown, I want you to be skeptical of them. The displacement is real. The people losing work right now are real people who built real skills over real years, and something has genuinely changed. I am not going to stand here and wave that away. It deserves to be taken seriously.

I have also seen how this story ends.

The printing press did not end writing. It ended the illusion that writing was only for the few who could afford a scribe. Is that fair? Photography did not kill painting - it freed painting from the obligation to be the only way a face could be preserved, and in doing so made painting more itself, more expressive, more free than it had ever been. Every tool that threatens a skill has ultimately elevated the people who had developed genuine discernment through that skill, and displaced only those who had learned the motion without ever building the understanding.

The skill was never the point. The skill was how you built what Aristotle called phronesis. He drew a hard line between three kinds of knowing: episteme, which is theoretical knowledge; techne, which is craft skill; and phronesis - practical wisdom. The ability to determine the right action in any particular, messy, ambiguous situation. Not just knowing the rule. Knowing when to apply it, when to bend it, and when to throw it out entirely because this moment is new and the rule was never built for it. Call it discernment, if you want a word that does not require footnotes. Same thing.

Discernment is not wisdom, exactly. Wisdom is broad - it accumulates about life. Discernment is narrower, more operational. And the opposite of discernment is not ignorance. The opposite of discernment is pattern-matching - knowing exactly what to do when conditions match something you have seen before, and having nothing to offer the moment they do not. A designer who learned the rules can execute any familiar brief perfectly. Give them a brief that is strange, contradictory, genuinely new - and watch what happens. They reach for the nearest template. Not because they are lazy, but because they learned the motion without ever earning the understanding beneath it.

An AI can pattern-match at a scale no human will ever touch. What it cannot do is know when the pattern does not apply. It cannot feel the thing that makes this situation different from the last ten thousand situations that looked similar on paper. That recognition - the felt sense of when to deviate, when the rule breaks, when something nobody has seen before is actually happening - that is phronesis. That is discernment. And it cannot be trained into existence on a dataset. It has to be lived into.

I have spent most of this past year building software alongside AI - not using it to replace the work, but working with it, in close conversation. And here is the thing I keep running into: it does not know when to stop. It will optimize toward whatever you asked for with complete commitment, right past the point where the goal itself was wrong. It does not know when the thing you asked for is technically correct and fundamentally a mistake. It will produce exactly what you said you wanted and miss entirely what you needed. That gap - between what you said and what you meant, between the literal request and the actual problem underneath it - is where discernment lives. And closing that gap costs something. It costs you having gotten it wrong enough times at enough cost to understand what right actually feels like in your gut. You cannot buy that. You cannot prompt it. And I think that is genuinely good news, if you are willing to think about your skills differently than you probably have been.

Don't mourn the tool. Invest that energy in deepening the thing the tool cannot touch.

The craft gets you in the room. The discernment determines whether you stay.

"The people who will outlast this moment are the ones who realize they were always in the discernment business. The craft was simply how they built it."

What happens when fear is the business model?

I have a theory about fear, and I have tested it carefully over forty-nine years.

The world is measurably safer - by nearly every quantifiable metric - than at any point in my lifetime. Violent crime is dramatically lower than its peak in the early nineties. Children are statistically safer from stranger danger than at any documented point in American history. Life expectancy is longer. Food, medicine, and knowledge are more accessible globally than they have ever been. These are not opinions. They are numbers.

And yet - the people around me are more afraid than I have ever seen them.

Here is why. The news does not give you the world. It gives you the extraordinary parts of the world - the outliers, the disasters, the rare and terrible events that are, by definition, unusual enough to mention. That is what news is for. Run that filter for twenty years without interruption and your sense of normal shifts. You do not notice it shifting. That is the point.

The cure is not less information. The cure is more life.

A shooting five states away becomes your lived reality because you encountered it eight times today. Your neighborhood, where nothing happened, does not make the feed. Your neighbor who brought you food when you were sick - that does not make the feed.

There are two kinds of people now, roughly. Those who live in their actual lives - who know their neighbors, who walk outside at night, who still make eye contact with a stranger on the street - and those who have let the feed become the world. I am not making a moral argument here. I am just observing that one of those groups is in contact with reality and the other one mostly is not.

The people I have met in every decade of my life who are genuinely at peace - not performing peace, but actually peaceful in their bodies - are almost always the ones who chose to stay curious about the world rather than afraid of it. They are not reckless. They are awake.

"The world is the safest it has ever been. Everyone is more afraid than ever. Those two facts are not a coincidence - they are a business model."

What is joy, if it is not a mood?

Let me tell you something I believe with everything I have.

Joy is not stimulation. Those are two different things and we have been confusing them for a while now. Stimulation is novelty stacked on novelty until your attention runs out. That is why so much of what gets called entertainment leaves you hollow within a week - sometimes within a day. You were not fed. You were occupied. There is a difference, and you can feel it in your body if you pay attention.

Joy is something you can return to. It grows when you tend it, the way a garden does. It leaves a mark.

I have spent years - honestly, decades - trying to understand what actually creates joy. In a life, in a game, in a relationship, in a piece of work that someone makes with genuine care. And I keep arriving at the same six things. These are what we try to build into every Haven Command product - from D4Chess, our live AI chess trainer, outward.

Connection

The moment you figure something out and your first instinct is to tell someone. The feeling of a shared world with shared rules. It is why multiplayer works, why a story works. Even a single-player game works when the person who made it cared - and you can feel that care coming right through the screen. That is connection.

Discovery

The first time you see something you did not know existed, and your brain simply says oh. The mechanic hidden in plain sight. The shortcut found by accident. The line of dialogue that reframes everything you thought you knew. The world revealing itself as larger and stranger than you imagined. Discovery is what the best games spend everything trying to create.

Understanding

When something clicks - the system reveals itself and you can suddenly see the rules. The journey from confusion to fluency. And here is what matters: that understanding separates you. Most people stop at the motion. The ones who push through to real comprehension carry something no tool can replicate. That is phronesis. That is discernment. That is what holds its value.

Purpose

Doing something that matters, even in a small imaginary context. Protecting something. Building something. Leaving a mark. The sense that your actions had weight - that they mattered to someone, even a fictional someone - is something people will play for hours just to feel. Purpose scales down gracefully. Even the smallest quest can carry it fully.

Presence

Not anticipating the next thing, not reviewing the last - actually in the moment where the thing is happening. Flow. Immersion. The state where you look up and an hour is gone. This is rare now. Modern life is engineered to interrupt you constantly. Games, when they truly work, cut through all of that and put you squarely in the present moment.

Safety

The foundation everything else stands on. You cannot be present, discover, or connect while in survival mode. Fear shuts down the very faculties that make play possible. Real joy requires enough safety to let you relax into the difficulty. This is not a design philosophy. This is physiology. Build the safety first. Everything else grows from it.

Now, here is what strikes me about all six. None of them require wealth. None of them require permission from anyone. All of them require presence. All of them require a world outside yourself - another person, a problem to solve, a genuine moment of contact with something that is not your own reflection.

Think about what a well-made game actually does. It manufactures the conditions for joy. It creates safe discovery - a world with real stakes that cannot actually harm you. It gives you purpose that feels genuine even when you know it is fictional. It earns connection between you and the world, between you and other players, between you and the human being who built the thing with their own hands.

I cannot design that experience from a spreadsheet. I have to have felt it first. The lived hours are not optional. This is what Haven Command is built on - not a framework, not a strategy document, not a pitch deck. The accumulated understanding of a person who has chased joy long enough to know what it actually is.

What are these Insights for?

I am writing here for the same reason I build games and software. Because I believe that the right idea, delivered to the right person at the right moment, can genuinely change the shape of a life. I have had that experience myself — more than once. I want to be on the giving end of it more often.

This is not a marketing channel. It is a place to think out loud about building things that matter, about running Haven Command LLC as a custom software and digital product studio with honesty, and about what it actually looks like to make something and stand behind it in public.

The notes that follow this essay are weeks after a build: what broke, what we decided, and what the experience became. They are not walkthroughs. Live products stay labeled live. Work still in progress stays labeled in development.

I have been building for most of my life. I am still in the middle of it. Start with what you love. Build the discernment. Protect your attention like the asset it is. Everything else tends to follow from those three.
